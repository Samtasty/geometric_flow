Sensitivity Analysis: Proficiency Noise

Dataset: attempts.csv  |  Truncate-at-round: 30  |  Epochs: 20  |  Noise: N(0, σ) added to proficiency θ before behavior fitting and training  |  Noise levels: σ ∈ {0.0 (baseline), 0.1, 0.2}

What This Experiment Tests

We inject Gaussian noise N(0,σ) into the proficiency estimates θ before behavior policy fitting and before learned policy training. This simulates measurement error in the context variable — e.g., if the KTM proficiency estimates are noisy or miscalibrated. The question is: how sensitive are the learned policies (and their off-policy evaluations) to this noise?

The behavior policy is also re-fitted with the noisy θ, so the entire pipeline (behavior fit, importance weights, training) is affected consistently. The baseline (σ=0.0) is the original attempts_trunc30 experiment.

Are Learned Policies Always Better Than Behavior?

Yes — at every noise level, every learned policy beats the behavior policy on every estimator. This holds for all four objectives (IPS, SNIPS, DR, MIS) and across σ ∈ {0.0, 0.1, 0.2}. The policy improvement signal is robust to proficiency noise.

Discussion

Learned Policies Always Beat Behavior

Across all three noise levels (σ = 0.0, 0.1, 0.2) and all five estimators (IPS, SNIPS, DR, MIS, DM), every learned policy outperforms the behavior policy. The policy improvement signal is robust to proficiency noise: even with substantial context corruption (σ=0.2 is ~14% of the difficulty range [−3.7, 2.6]), the policies still find meaningfully better difficulty assignments.

IPS Appears to Improve Under Noise — But This Is an Artifact

IPS values for learned policies increase with noise (Learned IPS: 3.125 → 3.169 → 3.214). This is not a genuine improvement. When proficiency is noisy, the behavior policy is fitted to a noisier context distribution — making it appear more diffuse (higher variance). A more diffuse behavior policy means that even when the learned policy picks a different action, the importance weight πlearned/πbehavior stays larger rather than collapsing. IPS is therefore upward biased under noise because the propensity denominator is inflated.

This is a known pathology: IPS without normalization is unreliable when the propensity model is misspecified.

SNIPS and DR Are the Most Informative Estimators

SNIPS and DR both show a consistent, moderate decline with noise (~8% and ~8% respectively at σ=0.2). This makes intuitive sense: noisier proficiency leads to noisier importance weights, and the self-normalized SNIPS estimator is more honest about policy quality than raw IPS. The decline is gradual rather than catastrophic, suggesting the pipeline is reasonably robust.

DM Is Remarkably Stable

The DM (Direct Method) estimator barely changes across noise levels (2.346 → 2.351 → 2.376 for the best policy). DM evaluates the learned reward model directly under the policy's action distribution, bypassing importance weights entirely. Since the reward model captures the true reward-difficulty-proficiency relationship and is only weakly sensitive to the exact proficiency value used during training, DM remains stable. This further validates DM as the most reliable estimator when importance weight variance is a concern.

Learned (IPS) vs Learned (DR) Under High Noise

An interesting reversal occurs at σ=0.2: Learned (DR)'s IPS score drops dramatically (2.846 → 2.256, −0.590) while Learned (IPS) stays high. This suggests the DR objective is more sensitive to proficiency noise in its gradient signal — the direct model component of DR becomes miscalibrated under noisy theta, leading the policy to different regions of the action space that look worse under IPS. However, Learned (DR) achieves the best DM score (2.376) at σ=0.2, suggesting it may still be finding a good policy that IPS just can't evaluate well.
